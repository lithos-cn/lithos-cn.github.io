## 0. What This Article Is About

Subtractive synthesis is how most analog synthesizers work — from the 1970 Minimoog to the software synths of today. The core idea fits in one sentence:

> Start with a harmonically rich sound produced by an oscillator, carve part of it away with a filter like a sculptor, then shape the volume over time with an amplifier driven by an envelope.

Three blocks and a handful of knobs. Once you understand them, you can program your own basses, pads, and leads instead of guessing at presets.

The basic signal flow of subtractive synthesis is:

```
Oscillator (VCO/DCO)  →  Filter (VCF)  →  Amplifier (VCA)  →  Output
        ↑                     ↑                 ↑
        └── LFO / Envelope ───┴── LFO / Envelope ─┘
```

> 📊 **Figure 0-1**: A horizontal signal-flow diagram: three main blocks (oscillator → filter → amplifier) joined by thick arrows; below them, two control sources (LFO and envelope generator) with thin dashed arrows pointing to the oscillator's "pitch", the filter's "cutoff", and the amplifier's "level", showing the idea of control signals modulating the audio signal.

## 1. Oscillator

### 1.1 The Four Basic Waveforms

The four most basic waveforms correspond to four different harmonic structures (a harmonic = a frequency component at an integer multiple of the fundamental):

| Waveform | Harmonic content | Sound |
|----------|------------------|-------|
| **Sine** | Fundamental only, no harmonics | The "purest", round tone, like a tuning fork |
| **Sawtooth** | All harmonics (odd + even), amplitude falls as 1/n | Bright, "buzzy" and metallic — the workhorse of strings, brass, and pads |
| **Square** | Odd harmonics only, amplitude falls as 1/n | Hollow, like a clarinet (a clarinet's cylindrical bore also favors odd harmonics) |
| **Triangle** | Odd harmonics only, amplitude falls as 1/n² | Softer than a square, between sine and square |

> 📊 **Figure 1-1**: Four rows side by side: on the left the waveform (time domain), on the right the corresponding spectrum (frequency vs. amplitude). The point is to see at a glance: sine has one bar; sawtooth has every harmonic tapering down; square has every other bar; triangle also has every other bar but falls off much faster.

A few key points worth expanding on:

- **What do "1/n" and "1/n²" mean?** In a sawtooth, the n-th harmonic's amplitude is about 1/n of the fundamental, so the 2nd harmonic is half the fundamental and the 10th is 1/10. In a triangle it's 1/n², so the 10th harmonic is only 1/100 of the fundamental — it falls off much faster. That's why a triangle sounds "dull" and a sawtooth sounds "bright": it's about how long the high-harmonic "tail" is.
- **Why does a square sound like a clarinet?** A clarinet's cylindrical bore physically suppresses even harmonics, so its tone is dominated by odd harmonics. A square wave's spectrum is also odd-harmonic dominant, hence the similarity. It's the clearest example of the "waveform → harmonics → timbre" chain.

### 1.2 DCO or VCO? A Common "Analog vs. Digital" Misunderstanding

- **VCO (Voltage-Controlled Oscillator)**: its frequency is set by an input control voltage — higher voltage → higher frequency. It's the classic, most "analog" approach. Its character actually comes from its instability: components drift with temperature, so the frequency wobbles slightly, creating a "living", organic detuning — what many call "analog warmth".
- **DCO (Digitally-Controlled Oscillator)**: it is still an analog oscillator — the waveform is produced by analog circuitry. The only difference is that its frequency is locked to a digital counter/clock reference, which fixes the tuning drift of early VCOs. Roland popularized the DCO with the Juno series in 1982, using a digital counter chip (like the 8253) as the frequency reference.

> 🎛️ **Producer's note**: I used to believe "DCO = digital oscillator" too. In reality, a DCO's waveform is still analog; only the "who decides the frequency" part is digital. To tell whether a synth is truly digital, look at whether the waveform itself is generated by digital samples/algorithms — not at who controls the frequency.

> 📊 **Figure 1-2**: A VCO vs. DCO comparison: VCO is "voltage → frequency" (a continuous voltage line directly controls it); DCO is "digital counter produces a reference pulse → analog circuitry still shapes the waveform". The key point is that the DCO's waveform core is still analog.

### 1.3 How the Waveforms Are Actually Made: Op-Amp Circuits

"How does a waveform get produced?" is the most interesting part of electronics. In a modern synth, the typical implementation combines two basic units built from op-amps:

1. **Schmitt trigger / comparator (a comparator with positive feedback)**: its output has only two states — positive or negative saturation, i.e. "high" or "low". It naturally produces a square wave.
2. **Integrator (an op-amp with a capacitor in the feedback path)**: it integrates the square wave. Since a square is "constant +V then constant −V", the integral is a linear ramp up then a linear ramp down — a triangle wave.

So the classic circuit is: Schmitt trigger outputs a square → integrator turns it into a triangle → the triangle feeds back to the Schmitt trigger's threshold, sustaining oscillation. One circuit gives you both a square and a triangle — two birds with one stone.

A sawtooth is similar, except the capacitor's charge and discharge rates are made asymmetric (fast one way, slow the other), giving one steep edge and one shallow ramp. To turn it into a VCO, you just make the charging current voltage-controlled: higher voltage → faster charge → steeper ramp → higher frequency. That is the physical essence of "voltage control".

> 📊 **Figure 1-3**: A block-level schematic of a triangle/square generator: comparator (labeled "Schmitt trigger, outputs square") → integrator (labeled "capacitor charge/discharge, outputs triangle") → a feedback arrow back to the comparator. Beside it, three small waveforms: the comparator's square, the integrator's triangle, and their time alignment.

### 1.4 "It Looks Clean, but the High End Sounds Dirty" — Harmonics and High Harmonics

This is the easiest place to get confused. On screen a sawtooth "looks clean", but listen closely and the top end is "furry". Two reasons:

1. An ideal saw/square has harmonics that decay very slowly (1/n), extending in theory to very high frequencies. That long high-frequency "tail" sounds harsh and "digital-cold".
2. In a digital system there's also aliasing: when harmonics exceed half the sample rate (the Nyquist frequency), they get "folded" back down, producing inharmonic, non-integer noise. That's why older digital synths have a certain "dirt" up high — it's not your imagination.

**How to fix it?** The filter in the next section. A low-pass filter shaves off the overly high harmonics, and the sound instantly becomes rounder and "warmer". This is exactly where the name subtractive synthesis comes from: start with a harmonic-rich waveform (saw/square), then "subtract" what you don't need.

> 🎛️ **Producer's note**: I used to think a "clean waveform" should give a "clean sound". Later I realized that visually "clean" (sharp edges) actually means "many, high harmonics" in the spectrum. A sound that is truly "clean" to the ear is one whose spectrum has been filtered down to just the fundamental and low harmonics.

## 2. The Low-Pass Filter (VCF)

### 2.1 The Filter Family: Which Slice Do You Cut?

Classified by "which part of the spectrum gets cut", the most common types are:

- **Low-Pass (a.k.a. High-Cut)**: lets lows through, cuts highs. The most used — it turns a harsh bright sound round.
- **High-Pass (Low-Cut)**: lets highs through, cuts lows. Used to remove muddy rumble.
- **Band-Pass**: keeps only a band in the middle, cutting both ends.
- **Notch / Band-Reject**: cuts only a narrow band in the middle, letting everything else through — the "opposite" of band-pass.

In subtractive synthesis, low-pass is the absolute mainstay, because our raw material (saw/square) is "too many harmonics, too bright on top" — exactly what we want to cut.

> 📊 **Figure 2-1**: Four side-by-side frequency-response diagrams: x-axis frequency (log), y-axis pass/attenuate. Low-pass is "passes on the left, slopes down on the right"; high-pass is the opposite; band-pass is "a hill in the middle"; notch is "a deep dip in the middle". Shade the "cut" region.

### 2.2 Cutoff: What Is the −3 dB Point?

A filter's cutoff frequency is the point where it "starts working". Engineers usually define cutoff as the frequency where the signal is attenuated by 3 dB.

- **−3 dB means half power**: voltage drops to about 0.707 (1/√2) and power to half. It's a conventional "effective boundary" — not a hard wall, but "from here on the attenuation becomes significant".
- A filter's attenuation isn't a vertical cliff; it has a slope measured in dB/octave. For example, a 12 dB/octave low-pass with cutoff at 440 Hz attenuates 880 Hz (one octave up) by 15 dB (3 + 12), and 1760 Hz by 27 dB, and so on. Steeper slope = cleaner cut.

> 📊 **Figure 2-2**: A single low-pass response curve: x-axis frequency, y-axis attenuation (dB), with a dot at the −3 dB point labeled "Cutoff (−3 dB point)". Draw a second, steeper curve for comparison to show "steeper slope = cuts harder".

### 2.3 Resonance: Making the Filter "Sing"

If you treat the filter as just a "treble knob", you're missing out. Resonance (also called Q, Emphasis, or Peak) feeds part of the filter's output back to its input, creating a peak around the cutoff:

- Turn up resonance and the cutoff gets emphasized, producing that "wow", "squeaky" resonant character — the soul of the TB-303 acid sound and countless leads.
- Push resonance high enough and many filters self-oscillate: they become a sine-wave generator in their own right, singing a pure tone at the cutoff. You can even use that as an extra oscillator.

> 📊 **Figure 2-3**: The same low-pass response, drawn at three resonance settings: low Q = smooth rolloff; mid Q = a small bump at the cutoff; high Q = a tall, sharp peak. Note "at high Q it can self-oscillate into a sine oscillator".

### 2.4 A Bit of Required History: the Moog Ladder Filter

The best example of "electronics + music history" overlapping is Bob Moog's ladder filter. In his 1965 patent, two mathematical cores defined what we later call "the Moog sound":

1. Four cascaded RC low-pass stages → a steep −24 dB/octave slope.
2. An output-to-input feedback path → resonance, and near the self-oscillation condition (Barkhausen criterion) the filter becomes a sine oscillator.

Add to that the soft clipping of the transistors (a tanh soft-limit nonlinearity), and together they make "the warm Moog sound". A single filter does both "cut the highs" and "color the tone" — this is the source of analog synths' charm: every module is an imperfect circuit that "pollutes" the signal, and that pollution is exactly what sounds good.

> 🎛️ **Producer's note**: I used to think of a filter as "an EQ". The difference: an EQ passively corrects the spectrum, while a synth's VCF (voltage-controlled filter) is part of sound design — its cutoff can move over time, with velocity, or with an LFO. A filter isn't a "processing effect"; it is the timbre itself.

## 3. Amplifier and Envelope

### 3.1 The Amplifier: Getting the Gain "Right"

The amplifier (usually a VCA, voltage-controlled amplifier) boosts the filter's output to audible level. The gain has to be appropriate:

- Too much gain and the signal peaks exceed what the circuit can handle linearly, causing clipping, then overdrive, even distortion.
- Clipping = the waveform's peaks get "flattened", and "flattening" is mathematically equivalent to adding new harmonics — so the sound gets brighter and dirtier.
- Unless that's exactly the effect you want — in fact many synths offer a Drive/Saturation knob specifically to turn this "overdrive" into a controllable timbre (see section 5).

> 📊 **Figure 3-1**: Three waveforms compared: a normally amplified sine (round peaks), light overdrive (slightly flattened peaks), and heavy distortion (almost a square). Next to each, a spectrum bar chart showing "the harder you clip, the more harmonics you get".

### 3.2 Why Do Envelopes Exist? — Starting from Acoustic Instruments

Before synthesizers offered this direct way of sculpting sound, people played acoustic instruments: percussion, strings, winds. Bound by physics, these instruments share common traits:

- Sound doesn't jump from 0 to maximum instantly (plucking, blowing, and bowing all take time);
- Sound doesn't drop to 0 instantly when you stop (strings, air columns, and drumheads all have decay).

To imitate them, you naturally have to imitate this "volume changing over time". Hence the envelope — it describes exactly the "life cycle" of a sound.

### 3.3 What Are A, D, S, and R?

The most common envelope model is ADSR, with four stages:

- **A (Attack)**: time for the volume to rise from 0 to maximum. Short = percussive (drums, plucks); long = swelling (strings, pads).
- **D (Decay)**: time to fall from the peak to the sustain level.
- **S (Sustain)**: the level held while the key stays pressed (note: this is a *level*, not a time). A wind instrument's held note corresponds to this stage.
- **R (Release)**: time for the volume to return to 0 after the key is released. Long release = lingering tail; short release = clean stop.

The key distinction: **A, D, R are times; S is a level.** This is the most common beginner confusion.

> 📊 **Figure 3-2**: A classic ADSR curve: x-axis time, y-axis volume. Draw the four stages in different colors: rising (A), falling (D), flat (S), and the falling section after release (R). Add a dashed line in the S stage to show "the level can be high or low".

### 3.4 A Bit of History: Where ADSR Came From

The four-stage ADSR shape was settled in the mid-1960s, with specific historical figures behind it:

- Composer Herbert Deutsch suggested Moog make the synth able to "articulate" rather than simply turn notes on and off. Moog initially even used a doorbell button and a capacitor to "store and slowly release" the voltage from a key press.
- Composer Vladimir Ussachevsky (then head of the Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center) explicitly asked Moog for an Attack-Decay-Sustain-Release four-stage voltage to control the amplifier (VCA). Moog built it as a module.

Since then, almost every synth envelope has used these four stages (sometimes with extras). One composer's request created a standard that persists to this day — a perfect example of "musical practice shaping technology".

### 3.5 Can an Envelope Only Control Volume? — the Filter Envelope

An envelope on the amplifier directly controls amplitude (volume). But it can also control the filter — that's the filter envelope:

- Route the same ADSR envelope to the filter's cutoff and the timbre's brightness changes over time.
- Classic example: a plucked string starts bright with rich high harmonics, then the high harmonics die away faster than the fundamental and the sound darkens. Sweeping the cutoff from high to low with an envelope convincingly simulates this "bright-to-dark" evolution.

So we get the two most common envelope routings: one controls "how loud" (VCA), one controls "how bright" (VCF). This is the source of "dynamic timbre" — a real instrument's tone is never static.

> 📊 **Figure 3-3**: A signal-flow diagram: one envelope generator connected to both the VCA's "level" and the VCF's "cutoff" with two dashed arrows. Beside it, two small plots: the top one is volume over time (the ADSR shape), the bottom is cutoff over time (a high-to-low decay).

## 4. LFO (Low-Frequency Oscillator)

### 4.1 First, the Range of Human Hearing

Human hearing spans roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz (in adults the practical upper limit is often 15–17 kHz):

- Below 20 Hz is infrasound — usually inaudible, though sometimes felt;
- Above 20 kHz is ultrasound — inaudible.

An LFO (low-frequency oscillator) is an oscillator tuned below the range of hearing (usually far below 20 Hz). Because you can't hear it, it isn't used to make sound — it's used to *control* other modules.

### 4.2 What Is Modulation?

In electronic music, modulation = using one signal to change some property of another signal. The signal you hear (being controlled) is the carrier; the signal issuing the command is the modulator.

The LFO is the most typical modulator. Route it to different targets and you get different classic effects:

- **LFO → oscillator pitch (frequency)** = vibrato — pitch wobbles up and down;
- **LFO → amplifier level (amplitude)** = tremolo — volume pulses;
- **LFO → filter cutoff** = a periodic "filter sweep" — the sound brightens and darkens (an auto-wah type effect).

> 📊 **Figure 4-1**: A "modulation routing" diagram: an LFO block in the middle with three dashed arrows to "pitch", "level", and "cutoff", each target labeled with its effect name (Vibrato / Tremolo / Sweep).

### 4.3 LFO Waveforms: How Are They the Same or Different from the Oscillator?

Same: an LFO uses the same waveforms as an audio oscillator — sine, triangle, square, saw, plus random (sample & hold), etc. Their mathematical shapes are identical.

Different:

| Dimension | Audio oscillator | LFO |
|-----------|------------------|-----|
| Frequency range | ~20 Hz – 20 kHz (audible) | Usually far below 20 Hz (inaudible) |
| Purpose | Produces the sound you hear (carrier) | Produces a signal that controls other modules (modulator) |
| What you care about in the waveform | Harmonic structure / timbre | The "shape of the change" (sine = smooth sway, square = jump back and forth, random = stepped jumps) |

So an LFO is basically "the same oscillator, slowed down a few hundred times, with a different job". This is one of the most charming things about modular synthesis: the same circuit unit changes identity when you change its frequency and its patch cables.

> 📊 **Figure 4-2**: A horizontal comparison: the top row shows four LFO waveforms (sine, triangle, square, random steps); below each, a small icon suggesting "how this waveform moves a knob" (sine = smooth back-and-forth, square = two positions, random = erratic).

### 4.4 Was the LFO Borrowed from Vibrato? (a Producer's Question)

> 🎛️ **Producer's note**: I've always suspected the LFO was borrowed from string players' vibrato — after all, vibrato on a violin is just periodic small pitch fluctuations.

The intuition is right, but the more accurate statement is: "periodically modulating pitch" itself far predates synthesizers. Violin and cello vibrato is the player rocking a finger back and forth on the string, periodically shortening and lengthening the effective string length and thus making pitch waver slightly — physically, that's frequency modulation (FM).

The LFO's significance is that it abstracted this "periodic motion done by a human hand" into a general-purpose, patchable circuit unit. On 1960s modular synths (like Moog), the LFO was even "accidental" at first — because modules could be freely patched, operators found that routing a slow signal to pitch produced vibrato. LFO and vibrato are two implementations of the same physical phenomenon: one from a human hand, one from a circuit.

> A small historical mix-up: a guitar's "tremolo arm" is misnamed by Leo Fender — moving the bar changes pitch (vibrato), whereas true tremolo is volume pulsing. So "vibrato = pitch, tremolo = volume" is still commonly confused today.

## 5. Effects

### 5.1 Effect Categories, Starting with "Spatial"

Effects can be roughly grouped into a few categories (not mutually exclusive; many span several):

1. **Spatial / time-based**: reverb, delay/echo. They create "space" — reverb simulates the tail of sound bouncing around a room; delay copies the signal, delays it, and repeats it.
2. **Modulation**: chorus, flanger, phaser, tremolo, rotary speaker, etc. They essentially use an LFO to vary delay time, gain, or phase, creating "movement" and "thickness".
3. **Distortion**: overdrive, distortion, fuzz. All three come from clipping, just to different degrees and in different ways.
4. **Dynamics**: compressor, limiter, expander, noise gate. They process the dynamic range of loudness, not timbre itself.
5. **Filter / EQ**: wah, EQ, filter effects. They adjust the spectrum.
6. **Pitch**: pitch shifter, octaver, harmonizer.

### 5.2 Is There an "Overdrive" Category? — Yes, and It's the Same Thing as Amplifier Overdrive

> 🎛️ **Producer's note**: I got stuck while outlining this — "is there really a dedicated overdrive category in effects? Or is it not counted because amplifier overdrive already exists?"

The answer: overdrive/distortion is not only a major effect category, it's the most important one in the guitar world. Overdrive, distortion, and fuzz all rely on clipping:

- **Clipping**: the signal peaks exceed what the circuit can handle and the peaks get "flattened".
- **Flattening = adding new harmonics**: the more the waveform approaches a square, the more odd harmonics — brighter and dirtier.
- The three differ in how and how much they clip: overdrive (soft clipping, gentle, dynamics preserved), distortion (harder, more compressed), fuzz (almost a square wave, buzzy sustain).

So amplifier overdrive (section 3.1) and effect distortion (this section) are fundamentally the same physical process — clipping — used at different points in the signal chain. The former is "the VCA inside the synth pushed too hard"; the latter is "a dedicated, controllable clipping effect". This is also why many synths have both VCA drive and a dedicated distortion unit in the effects: same thing, two entry points, one inside the engine and one outside it.

### 5.3 Guitar Effect Chains vs. Synths

Guitarists are extremely particular about effect order, while synths bake many "effects" into the sound engine itself. Putting them side by side is revealing.

The classic guitar pedal order (a rule of thumb, not absolute):

```
Tuner → dynamics/wah → overdrive/distortion/fuzz (low to high gain) → modulation (chorus/flanger/phaser) → delay → reverb
```

The logic behind the order:

1. **Distortion goes before modulation and spatial effects**: so you're modulating an already-distorted sound; reversed, the modulation sweep would be clipped and reshaped by the drive — different, not wrong, just a choice.
2. **Delay/reverb go last**: because they "copy" the signal. If distortion came after delay, every echo copy would be clipped separately — volume jumps and mud.

Synths vs. guitars:

| Dimension | Guitar | Synth |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| Signal source | String + pickup (the signal is "given") | Oscillator (you choose the waveform freely) |
| The filter's role | Wah/EQ is an effect, optional | The VCF is the core of the engine, it *is* the timbre |
| Envelope | Playing dynamics naturally provide attack/decay | ADSR is explicit and precisely controllable |
| Where overdrive comes from | Amp/effect (pushed preamp) | VCA internal drive + external distortion effect |
| Effect chain | A string of external pedals in series | Some built into the engine (VCF/envelope/LFO), some external (reverb/delay) |

The shared underlying logic: both guitar pedals and synths use the same signal-processing building blocks (filtering, clipping, delay, modulation) — they're just organized differently. A guitar runs "a chain" after the instrument; a synth sews part of that chain into its sound-producing structure. Once you see this, you understand both.

## 6. FAQ

- **A sine has no harmonics — why keep it?** It's the "purest raw material", used for sub-bass layering, self-oscillating filters, FM synthesis, etc.; in pure subtractive synthesis it's rarely a main timbre on its own.
- **Why does a "clean-looking" waveform sound "dirty"?** Sharp edges = many high harmonics in the spectrum; a truly "clean" sound needs the filter to shave those off.
- **Is a DCO a digital oscillator?** No. Its waveform core is still analog; only the frequency reference is digital.
- **What does −3 dB actually mean?** The half-power point — the conventional "cutoff" boundary.
- **What happens if you max out resonance?** Many filters self-oscillate and become a sine-wave generator.
- **Can an envelope only control volume?** No. The second most common routing goes to filter cutoff (the filter envelope), controlling how brightness changes over time.
- **What's the difference between an LFO and an audio oscillator?** Essentially the same thing, just tuned below hearing and used to control other modules.
- **Vibrato vs. tremolo — which is which?** Vibrato = pitch fluctuation; tremolo = volume fluctuation. A guitar's "tremolo arm" is actually misnamed by Fender; it does vibrato.
